# Truck Accident Litigation Investigation Workflow

## Purpose

This document summarizes how experienced truck accident litigation attorneys investigate motor carriers, drivers, and commercial vehicle accidents. The information is based on trucking litigation practice guides and attorney investigation checklists.

The purpose of this knowledge is to help the Carrier Intelligence Portal understand:

* What lawyers investigate
* Why they investigate it
* What information they need
* What information is difficult to obtain
* How a Carrier Intelligence Report can provide value

---

# Core Objective of Truck Litigation

In a truck accident case, lawyers generally attempt to answer four questions:

1. Was the driver qualified to operate the commercial vehicle?
2. Was the motor carrier negligent?
3. Did the motor carrier know about safety problems before the accident?
4. Can the lawyer prove those facts with documentary evidence?

The investigation process is designed to answer these questions.

---

# Driver Qualification Investigation

Lawyers investigate whether the driver was properly qualified and legally permitted to operate the vehicle.

Typical areas of investigation include:

* CDL history
* License suspensions
* License revocations
* Prior accidents
* Prior traffic violations
* Driver training records
* Medical certifications
* Drug and alcohol testing history
* Prior employment history
* Safety performance with previous employers

The primary source is the Driver Qualification File (DQF).

A weak DQF may support claims for:

* Negligent hiring
* Negligent retention
* Negligent supervision
* Negligent entrustment

---

# Motor Carrier Investigation

Lawyers investigate whether the trucking company maintained adequate safety management systems.

Typical areas of investigation include:

* Hiring procedures
* Driver screening procedures
* Safety policies
* Training programs
* Maintenance programs
* Hours-of-Service compliance
* Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulation (FMCSR) compliance

The objective is to determine whether the carrier failed to meet industry standards.

---

# Pattern of Misconduct Investigation

One of the most important objectives is establishing a pattern of misconduct.

Lawyers are generally less interested in a single violation and more interested in recurring problems.

Examples include:

* Multiple crashes
* Repeated Hours-of-Service violations
* Repeated Out-of-Service violations
* Repeated maintenance violations
* Repeated driver qualification failures
* Repeated insurance cancellations
* Repeated authority revocations

A documented pattern can support claims for:

* Corporate negligence
* Reckless conduct
* Punitive damages

---

# Electronic Evidence Investigation

Modern truck accident litigation relies heavily on electronic evidence.

Common sources include:

* Electronic Logging Devices (ELD)
* Engine Control Module (ECM) downloads
* GPS and telematics systems
* Qualcomm messaging systems
* Cell phone records
* Dashcam footage
* Collision avoidance systems
* Internal emails
* Social media postings

These records are often used to establish:

* Driver fatigue
* Speed
* Braking activity
* Driver distraction
* Route history
* Company knowledge of safety issues

---

# FMCSA and SAFER Investigation

Truck accident lawyers routinely investigate public FMCSA data.

Common sources include:

* SAFER
* Licensing & Insurance records
* SMS/BASIC scores
* Inspection history
* Crash history
* Authority records

However, these sources are fragmented and require manual investigation.

Lawyers frequently need to:

* Visit multiple FMCSA systems
* Download records individually
* Review inspection histories
* Review crash histories
* Review authority records
* Review insurance records

This process is time-consuming and often requires significant manual analysis.

---

# Information Lawyers Ultimately Want

Lawyers are not merely collecting records.

They are attempting to answer strategic questions.

Examples include:

* Was the carrier legally operating on the accident date?
* Was insurance active on the accident date?
* Were there prior insurance cancellations?
* Were there prior authority revocations?
* Was the carrier experiencing financial distress?
* Was safety performance improving or deteriorating?
* Did the carrier have a history of crashes?
* Did the carrier have a history of regulatory violations?
* Was the driver properly screened and qualified?
* Did the carrier ignore known safety issues?

These questions are often not directly answered by FMCSA systems.

---

# Opportunity for Carrier Intelligence Reports

Traditional FMCSA and SAFER systems provide data.

They do not provide conclusions.

The Carrier Intelligence Report should focus on transforming fragmented data into actionable intelligence.

Rather than simply displaying records, the report should answer questions.

Examples:

* Insurance active on accident date: Yes / No
* Prior insurance cancellations: Count and dates
* Prior authority revocations: Count and dates
* Crash history summary
* Fatal crash history
* Inspection trends
* Out-of-Service trends
* Safety score trends
* Fleet size trends
* Distress indicators
* Litigation risk indicators

The objective is to provide lawyers with immediate insight rather than requiring them to perform manual analysis.

# Recommended Report Structure

## Carrier Profile

* Carrier name
* DOT number
* MC number
* Fleet size
* Driver count
* Years in operation
* Safety rating

## Safety Timeline

* Inspections
* Violations
* Out-of-Service events
* BASIC score history

## Authority Timeline

* Authority granted
* Revocations
* Reinstatements

## Insurance Timeline

* Insurance filings
* Cancellations
* Coverage gaps

## Crash Timeline

* Fatal crashes
* Injury crashes
* Tow-away crashes

## Distress Analysis

* Insurance instability
* Authority instability
* Fleet decline
* Safety deterioration

## Litigation Red Flags

* Driver qualification concerns
* Maintenance violations
* Hours-of-Service violations
* Drug and alcohol violations
* Repeat crash patterns
* Repeat safety violations

# Key Product Insight

Truck accident lawyers already investigate FMCSA data as part of their normal workflow.

The problem is not access to data.

The problem is that the data is fragmented, difficult to interpret, and requires significant manual effort to transform into useful litigation intelligence.

The Carrier Intelligence Portal should focus on solving this problem by converting raw FMCSA records into concise, actionable, litigation-focused insights.
